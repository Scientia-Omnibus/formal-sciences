## Use Geoboards to Model Slope


In this section, we will explore the concepts of slope.

Using rubber bands on a **geoboard** gives a concrete way to model lines on a coordinate grid. By stretching a rubber band between two pegs on a geoboard, we can discover how to find the slope of a line.

We’ll start by stretching a rubber band between two pegs to make a line as shown in .

Does it look like a line?

Now we stretch one part of the rubber band straight up from the left peg and around a third peg to make the sides of a right triangle as shown in . We carefully make a <span class="math-inline">90°</span> angle around the third peg, so that one side is vertical and the other is horizontal.

To find the slope of the line, we measure the distance along the vertical and horizontal legs of the triangle. The vertical distance is called the ***rise*** and the horizontal distance is called the ***run***, as shown in .

To help remember the terms, it may help to think of the images shown in .

On our geoboard, the rise is <span class="math-inline">2</span> units because the rubber band goes up <span class="math-inline">2</span> spaces on the vertical leg. See .

What is the run? Be sure to count the spaces between the pegs rather than the pegs themselves! The rubber band goes across <span class="math-inline">3</span> spaces on the horizontal leg, so the run is <span class="math-inline">3</span> units.

The slope of a line is the ratio of the rise to the run. So the slope of our line is <span class="math-inline"><sup>2</sup>∕<sub>3</sub>.</span> In mathematics, the slope is always represented by the letter <span class="math-inline">m.</span>


<div class="definition-label">**Slope of a line**</div>


The slope of a line is <span class="math-inline">m=<sup>rise</sup>∕<sub>run</sub>.</span>

The **rise** measures the vertical change and the **run** measures the horizontal change.

When we work with geoboards, it is a good idea to get in the habit of starting at a peg on the left and connecting to a peg to the right. Then we stretch the rubber band to form a right triangle.

If we start by going up the rise is positive, and if we stretch it down the rise is negative. We will count the run from left to right, just like you read this paragraph, so the run will be positive.

Since the slope formula has rise over run, it may be easier to always count out the rise first and then the run.

## Find the Slope of a Line from its Graph


Now we’ll look at some graphs on a **coordinate grid** to find their slopes. The method will be very similar to what we just modeled on our geoboards.

To find the slope, we must count out the **rise** and the **run**. But where do we start?

We locate any two points on the line. We try to choose points with coordinates that are integers to make our calculations easier. We then start with the point on the left and sketch a right triangle, so we can count the rise and run.

## Find the Slope of Horizontal and Vertical Lines


Do you remember what was special about horizontal and vertical lines? Their equations had just one variable.

- horizontal line <span class="math-inline">y=b;</span> all the <span class="math-inline">y</span>-coordinates are the same.

- vertical line <span class="math-inline">x=a;</span> all the <span class="math-inline">x</span>-coordinates are the same.

So how do we find the slope of the horizontal line <span class="math-inline">y=4?</span> One approach would be to graph the horizontal line, find two points on it, and count the rise and the run. Let’s see what happens in . We’ll use the two points <span class="math-inline">(0, 4)</span> and <span class="math-inline">(3, 4)</span> to count the rise and run.


The slope of the horizontal line <span class="math-inline">y=4</span> is <span class="math-inline">0.</span>

All horizontal lines have slope <span class="math-inline">0</span>. When the <span class="math-inline">y</span>-coordinates are the same, the rise is <span class="math-inline">0</span>.


<div class="definition-label">**Slope of a Horizontal Line**</div>


The **slope** of a **horizontal line**, <span class="math-inline">y=b,</span> is <span class="math-inline">0.</span>

Now we’ll consider a vertical line, such as the line <span class="math-inline">x=3</span>, shown in . We’ll use the two points <span class="math-inline">(3, 0)</span> and <span class="math-inline">(3, 2)</span> to count the rise and run.


But we can’t divide by <span class="math-inline">0.</span> Division by <span class="math-inline">0</span> is undefined. So we say that the slope of the vertical line <span class="math-inline">x=3</span> is undefined. The slope of all vertical lines is undefined, because the run is <span class="math-inline">0.</span>


<div class="definition-label">**Slope of a Vertical Line**</div>


The **slope** of a **vertical line**, <span class="math-inline">x=a,</span> is undefined.

## Use the Slope Formula to find the Slope of a Line between Two Points


Sometimes we need to find the slope of a line between two points and we might not have a graph to count out the rise and the run. We could plot the points on grid paper, then count out the rise and the run, but there is a way to find the slope without graphing.

Before we get to it, we need to introduce some new algebraic notation. We have seen that an **ordered pair** <span class="math-inline">(x,y)</span> gives the coordinates of a point. But when we work with slopes, we use two points. How can the same symbol <span class="math-inline">(x,y)</span> be used to represent two different points?

Mathematicians use **subscripts** to distinguish between the points. A subscript is a small number written to the right of, and a little lower than, a variable.

- <span class="math-inline">(x<sub>1</sub>,y<sub>1</sub>) read x sub 1,y sub 1</span>

- <span class="math-inline">(x<sub>2</sub>,y<sub>2</sub>) read x sub 2,y sub 2</span>

We will use <span class="math-inline">(x<sub>1</sub>,y<sub>1</sub>)</span> to identify the first point and <span class="math-inline">(x<sub>2</sub>,y<sub>2</sub>)</span> to identify the second point. If we had more than two points, we could use <span class="math-inline">(x<sub>3</sub>,y<sub>3</sub>),(x<sub>4</sub>,y<sub>4</sub>),</span> and so on.

To see how the rise and run relate to the coordinates of the two points, let’s take another look at the slope of the line between the points <span class="math-inline">(2,3)</span> and <span class="math-inline">(7,6)</span> in .

## Graph a Line Given a Point and the Slope


In this chapter, we graphed lines by plotting points, by using intercepts, and by recognizing horizontal and vertical lines.

Another method we can use to graph lines is the **point-slope method**. Sometimes, we will be given one point and the slope of the line, instead of its equation. When this happens, we use the definition of slope to draw the graph of the line.


<div class="example">
**Example**

Plot the given point, <span class="math-inline">(1,−1).</span>


Use the slope formula <span class="math-inline">m=<sup>rise</sup>∕<sub>run</sub></span> to identify the rise and the run.
\(\\
 \\
 \\
\frac{rise}{run} \\
 \\
 \\
 \\\)
Starting at the point we plotted, count out the rise and run to mark the second point. We count <span class="math-inline">3</span> units up and <span class="math-inline">4</span> units right.

We can check our line by starting at any point and counting up <span class="math-inline">3</span> and to the right <span class="math-inline">4.</span> We should get to another point on the line.

## Key Concepts


- *Find the slope from a graph*
  - Locate two points on the line whose coordinates are integers.
  - Starting with the point on the left, sketch a right triangle, with the hypotenuse going from the first point to the second point.
  - Count the rise and the run on the legs of the triangle.
  - Take the ratio of rise to run to find the slope, <span class="math-inline">m=<sup>rise</sup>∕<sub>run</sub></span>

- *Slope of a Horizontal Line*
  - The slope of a horizontal line, <span class="math-inline">y=b</span>, is 0.

- *Slope of a Vertical Line*
  - The slope of a vertical line, <span class="math-inline">x=a</span>, is undefined.

- *Slope Formula*
  - The slope of the line between two points <span class="math-inline">(x<sub>1</sub>,y<sub>1</sub>)</span> and <span class="math-inline">(x<sub>2</sub>,y<sub>2</sub>)</span> is <span class="math-inline">m=<sup>y<sub>2</sub>-y<sub>1</sub></sup>∕<sub>x<sub>2</sub>-x<sub>1</sub></sub></span>

- *Graph a line given a point and a slope.*
  - Plot the given point.
  - Use the slope formula to identify the rise and the run.
  - Starting at the given point, count out the rise and run to mark the second point.
  - Connect the points with a line.
